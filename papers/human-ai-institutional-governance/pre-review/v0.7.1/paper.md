# Abstract

Artificial intelligence can participate in institutional processes by assisting the formulation of proposals, analysis of alternatives, retrieval of institutional records, contestation of claims, and execution of bounded operational tasks. These capabilities raise a governance question: how can institutions use distributed cognitive and operational capabilities while preserving legitimately constituted authority, meaningful participation, accountability, and the capacity to challenge decisions?

This paper proposes an integrative conceptual framework for examining AI-assisted governance in human institutions. Its normative premise is that institutional authority must be constituted through legitimate human institutional arrangements; technical capability or operational performance does not independently establish such authority. The framework distinguishes four substantive dimensions—human legitimacy, institutional intelligence, agentic delegation, and federated institutional autonomy—from three analytical levels: institutional principles and authority, governance processes and human–AI interaction, and technical implementation mechanisms.

Institutional intelligence is treated as a set of organizational functions rather than as a claim that intelligence is generated exclusively by artificial agents. AI assistance may be persistent, episodic, specialized, or distributed across institutions. The paper examines proposal composition, deliberative analysis, contestation, and institutional memory, together with the risks of agenda influence, cognitive dependence, shared bias, and false interpretive authority.

As a cross-cutting analytical device, the paper develops an *Institutional Decision Episode* that distinguishes the validity of an institutional mandate, the conformity of a delegated action, and the practical capacity to contest or correct an outcome. A hypothetical procurement episode illustrates how these categories can support different lines of inquiry. The example does not demonstrate improved governance or operational effectiveness. The framework is a normative and conceptual synthesis; its usefulness, effectiveness, and applicability across institutional forms remain to be evaluated.

**Keywords:** institutional governance; human authority; institutional intelligence; AI delegation; accountability; contestability; socio-technical systems

# 1. Introduction

Institutions increasingly use computational systems to support activities that extend beyond record storage and the execution of fixed procedures. Contemporary artificial intelligence systems can generate text, summarize documents, identify possible relationships among records, suggest alternatives, and operate software tools when connected to suitable interfaces. These capabilities create opportunities to redistribute some of the cognitive and operational work involved in governing an organization.

The institutional implications extend beyond whether an automated service should be allowed to perform a specific task. Governance also depends on how problems enter an agenda, how proposals are composed, what information reaches decision-makers, how past commitments are interpreted, and whether affected parties can question an outcome. AI systems may influence each of these activities even when they do not possess a formal vote or final decision right.

This paper asks:

> How can human institutions incorporate AI into their governance processes to expand their cognitive and operational capacities while preserving legitimate authority, meaningful participation, accountability, and institutional autonomy?

The question is normative and organizational as well as technical. A system’s ability to generate a proposal, operate a tool, or influence a decision does not, by itself, explain whether the resulting action is institutionally authorized. Conversely, limiting formal decision rights to humans does not establish that human participants have sufficient information, time, access, or practical influence to exercise those rights meaningfully.

The framework begins from a **human-centered normative premise**: within the institutional arrangements examined here, authority is to be constituted through legitimate human principals and human institutions. AI systems may contribute to institutional cognition and may perform operational actions where authorized, but their cognitive or technical capabilities do not independently confer institutional sovereignty. This premise is a starting point for argument and institutional design, not an empirical finding or a universal legal conclusion.

The paper develops an integrative account around four dimensions:

1. **Human legitimacy:** the basis on which institutional authority is constituted, exercised, delegated, challenged, and amended.
2. **Institutional intelligence:** the human and AI-assisted cognitive functions involved in composing proposals, analyzing alternatives, contesting claims, and accessing institutional memory.
3. **Agentic delegation:** the relationship among institutional mandates, operational capabilities, technical permissions, actions, and accountability.
4. **Federated institutional autonomy:** how distinct institutions may organize their own capabilities while remaining subject to applicable shared commitments.

These dimensions identify subjects of investigation. They are complemented by three analytical levels that distinguish **institutional authority**, **governance processes**, and **technical mechanisms**. The two structures are not interchangeable: one identifies substantive concerns, while the other separates types of institutional analysis.

The paper also uses an *Institutional Decision Episode* as a cross-cutting device. It connects cognitive work before a decision to the mandate under which action occurs, the action itself, the evidence available, and the possibility of review or correction. This device integrates established concerns about authority, delegation, accountability, and contestability; it is not presented as the discovery of those concerns.

The framework is intended for conceptual examination across different organizational forms. Its portability is a research proposition, not an established result. Blockchain, smart contracts, tokenization, or any other specific implementation technology are optional possibilities rather than premises of the analysis.

# 2. Theoretical Foundations and Conceptual Method

## 2.1 Socio-technical institutions and distributed governance

A socio-technical perspective treats institutional outcomes as arising from interactions among people, organizational rules, practices, information, and technical systems. Baxter and Sommerville (2011) argue that technical design must account for organizational and social conditions rather than treating them as external to systems engineering. This perspective is relevant to AI-assisted governance because the consequences of a model output depend on how it is introduced into a workflow, who can question it, and which actors possess authority to act on it.

Systems-oriented safety analysis likewise directs attention to interactions and control relationships rather than isolated component failures alone (Leveson, 2012). In the present paper, this insight is used as a conceptual caution: failures of institutional oversight may involve mismatches among mandates, organizational practices, available information, and technical execution. It does not establish that the proposed framework prevents such failures.

Polycentric governance provides a further reference for examining institutions with multiple, interdependent centers of decision-making (Ostrom, 2010). Some organizations may be federated or otherwise distributed; others may use different structures. Polycentricity is therefore a useful comparative perspective, not a requirement that all AI-assisted institutions share a constitutional center or federal hierarchy.

## 2.2 Legitimacy, decision rights, and delegated action

A distinction between technically possible action and legitimate exercise of authority is central to the proposed framework. Stone and Mittelstadt examine the legitimacy of automated exercises of power and caution against reducing legitimacy to other desirable properties, such as accuracy or procedural performance (Stone & Mittelstadt, 2026). Their work provides an important point of comparison: compliance with a procedure may itself require scrutiny of the authority and institutional conditions under which that procedure operates.

Organizational research also examines how decision rights are distributed among human and AI participants. Sebastian et al. (2026) discuss decision rights for AI in an organizational context. Engin (2026) examines configurations of decision authority, process autonomy, and accountability. These approaches overlap materially with the distinctions pursued here. The present paper does not claim to have originated the separation between authority and operational autonomy.

The more specific question developed below concerns how an institutional episode can be examined when its mandate, execution, and review conditions do not align. This is proposed as an organizing synthesis. Whether it produces a substantive contribution beyond existing frameworks remains a matter for further comparison.

## 2.3 Human control, reviewability, and contestability

Meaningful human control cannot be assumed merely because a person appears somewhere in a decision process. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) analyze conditions under which autonomous systems can remain connected to human reasons and responsibility. Their account is relevant to the distinction between nominal human involvement and a substantively meaningful relationship between human agency and system behavior.

Research on the oversight of automated decisions also questions policies that treat a human review step as sufficient by default (Green, 2022). The relevance here is not that every human reviewer will fail, but that oversight depends on institutional conditions: access to information, practical capacity, authority to intervene, and an appropriate understanding of the task.

Accountability and contestability scholarship adds a complementary concern. Kroll et al. (2017) argue that transparency alone is not an adequate account of algorithmic accountability. Cobbe et al. (2021) examine the reviewability of automated decision-making as a socio-technical process. Alfrink et al. (2023) develop contestability as a design concern. Together, these works support examining how decisions can be reconstructed, questioned, and reconsidered; they do not establish that a particular record or review procedure necessarily produces an effective remedy.

## 2.4 Related work and the status of the contribution

The proposed framework draws together ideas already present in scholarship on institutional authority, AI governance, meaningful human control, reviewability, and contestability. It should be understood as an **integrative conceptual synthesis**, not as a claim to have invented these fields.

The institutional decision episode is intended to make relationships among mandate validity, delegated-action conformity, and contestability explicit within a common analytical unit. These distinctions overlap with prior work, including the sources above. The available R1 Foundations record also identified Kang’s (2026) *Institutional Judgment Governance* as a potentially close comparator. Because the full text was not established as accessible in the prior assessment, no claim is made here that Kang’s work lacks the distinctions developed in this paper. Further comparison would be required before advancing a stronger claim of distinctiveness.

## 2.5 Conceptual method

The paper uses **normative-conceptual analysis and a bounded integrative synthesis**. It does not report a systematic literature review, empirical experiment, formal proof, or implementation study.

The method has three components.

First, it separates concepts that are often conflated in discussions of AI governance: institutional authority, cognitive assistance, factual agency, technical permission, institutional authorization, responsibility, and contestability.

Second, it organizes these concerns through four substantive dimensions and three analytical levels. The proposed structure is intended to make relationships and possible tensions inspectable. It is not claimed to be necessary, minimal, uniquely correct, or universally sufficient.

Third, it uses a hypothetical decision episode to examine whether several kinds of institutional failure can be distinguished without assuming that each failure has the same cause or remedy. The episode is an illustration of conceptual analysis. It cannot establish how frequently such failures occur or whether the framework improves real institutional outcomes.

# 3. Legitimate Authority and Institutional Decision Rights

## 3.1 Human-centered authority as a normative premise

The framework proceeds from the normative position that institutional authority should be constituted through legitimate human institutional arrangements. Depending on the institution, those arrangements may involve individuals, representative bodies, boards, associations, public authorities, or collective decision procedures.

This premise has two implications for the argument. First, an AI system’s ability to analyze, persuade, coordinate, or execute does not **by itself** establish that it holds independent institutional authority. Second, a human institution cannot establish the legitimacy of its authority merely by describing a technical mechanism that carries out its decisions. The relevant institutional basis must itself be identified and may be subject to contestation.

The framework does **not** purport to settle universal questions about whether an AI system could receive legal personhood, rights, or institutional standing under a particular legal regime. Those questions vary by jurisdiction and remain outside the conclusions of this paper. Likewise, what constitutes a legitimate principal in a particular organization requires reference to its applicable rules and, where relevant, legal context.

A working analytical definition is therefore deliberately limited:

> A **recognized institutional principal or body** is an actor or collective body treated, under the applicable institutional arrangements being examined, as holding or exercising a specified decision right.

Recognition under a stated rule is an object of analysis; it does not automatically settle the deeper legitimacy of that rule. The relationship between human legitimacy, collective institutions, and legally recognized entities remains an open normative and domain question.

## 3.2 Capability, permission, factual agency, and authority

Four concepts should be distinguished.

| Concept | Working meaning | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical capability** | What a system can materially compute, generate, access, or execute in an environment. | That any action is permitted or legitimate. |
| **Technical permission** | What a configured mechanism allows a system or credential to invoke. | That the configuration reflects a valid institutional mandate. |
| **Operational agency** | The factual capacity to act or influence events, including by initiating tool calls or shaping available information. | That the action was authorized or that the actor holds legitimate institutional authority. |
| **Institutional authority** | A decision right or competence constituted or recognized under the institutional arrangements being examined. | That the right is technically enforceable or uncontested in every relevant context. |

Operational agency is a **factual** concept in this model. A system may act outside its mandate, use an overly broad technical permission, or perform an action without any valid institutional authorization. It may also influence a decision through agenda selection or presentation of information without possessing an explicit execution permission.

The distinction matters because an access-control mechanism may permit an action that the institution did not validly authorize. It also matters in the opposite direction: an institution may intend to authorize an action, while the technical system is unable or incorrectly configured to perform it. In neither case does the observed technical state settle all institutional questions.

An action lacking a valid mandate should therefore be classified initially as an **authority or authorization concern** under the framework. Whether it is institutionally invalid, legally void, voidable, capable of ratification, or binding on an external party depends on the applicable rules and circumstances. The model does not presume a universal legal consequence.

## 3.3 Candidate questions about delegation and retained authority

The human-centered premise does not require direct human approval of every operational act. An institution may wish to authorize bounded automation while retaining authority over the mandate, its limits, and the procedures for review or amendment.

Which powers may be delegated, and which must remain reserved to human institutional bodies, is not resolved here. Five categories deserve particular institutional examination:

1. **Constitutive and amendment powers:** authority to establish or revise foundational rules.
2. **Allocation of institutional competence:** authority to create roles, grant mandates, or alter their scope.
3. **Participation and standing:** authority to determine membership, voting rights, or access to institutional procedures.
4. **Adjudication and remedy:** authority to issue binding dispositions on disputes or challenges.
5. **Emergency powers and exceptions:** authority to suspend ordinary procedures or exercise exceptional powers.

These are **candidate areas for determination**, not a universally established list of nondelegable powers. Their treatment may differ by institution, applicable law, risk, reversibility, and the nature of the decision. The distinction between authority over a standing mandate and approval of individual actions within that mandate remains central to the open question of Human Finality.

## 3.4 Responsibility without automatic individualization

Accountability requires more than assigning a name to an outcome. Institutional responsibility may involve a collective body that established a mandate, people who configured a process, operators who supervised it, and reviewers or other parties with relevant duties. Which roles bear responsibility depends on the institution’s rules and the facts of the episode.

The framework therefore seeks **attributable institutional responsibility** rather than presuming that every outcome must be assigned to one individual. Attribution for governance analysis is also distinct from a determination of legal liability.

# 4. AI-Augmented Institutional Intelligence

## 4.1 Institutional intelligence as a governance function

*Institutional intelligence* refers here to the capabilities through which an institution gathers, preserves, interprets, questions, and uses information in governing itself. These capabilities arise from people, practices, records, and technical systems. AI may augment them, but institutional intelligence should not be equated with AI-generated output.

The proposed model distinguishes four functions:

- **Composition:** turning an institutional intention or concern into a proposal that others can understand and deliberate on.
- **Analysis:** examining assumptions, alternatives, consequences, and relevant institutional context.
- **Contestation:** generating or supporting challenges to proposals, interpretations, evidence, and decisions.
- **Memory:** making prior records, commitments, and decisions available for current institutional work.

An institution could organize these functions in many ways. AI assistance might be continuous, occasional, specialized, centrally provided, or distributed among participants. A permanent AI layer is therefore a possible arrangement, not a condition of the framework.

The value of these functions is a question for evaluation. AI may expand the analytical resources available to participants, but it may also introduce errors, dependency, or uneven influence. The analysis below treats benefits as possibilities and design objectives, not observed results.

## 4.2 Proposal composition and institutional participation

Proposal composition is an institutional process because it determines how a concern becomes an object of collective attention and potential decision. A proposal must normally be intelligible to the relevant participants and connected to a process through which they can consider it.

AI assistance may help a participant clarify an objective, identify missing information, organize arguments, or locate potentially relevant rules. It may also offer alternative formulations or explain what further questions a proposal leaves unresolved. These functions could lower some drafting barriers for some participants; whether they do so, and for whom, requires evaluation.

Composition is not a neutral transcription of a participant’s intention. A system may suggest objectives, select a problem definition, omit alternatives, or embed assumptions about what is feasible. The participant and the institution therefore need ways to distinguish the participant’s stated purpose from AI-generated additions or reframing.

Wider access to composition assistance may support participation, but access alone does not establish equal understanding, institutional standing, influence over an agenda, or political power. Differences in resources, experience, accessibility, and control over the tools may persist or change form.

## 4.3 Analysis and deliberative assistance

Institutional deliberation often requires participants to compare alternatives under incomplete information. AI systems may help summarize source materials, identify apparent inconsistencies, assemble questions about consequences, or present competing interpretations for examination.

The institutional role of analysis is to support deliberation, not to convert a model-generated assessment into a binding disposition. A useful analysis should allow participants to distinguish:

1. the source records or evidence consulted;
2. the interpretation or inference generated from those records;
3. assumptions and unresolved uncertainties;
4. alternatives that were considered or omitted; and
5. the body or role competent to decide the relevant issue.

These are proposed conditions for reviewable assistance. They are not a claim that every system can provide a complete explanation or that source links alone guarantee correctness.

Analytical assistance also has distributional consequences. The same summary can help participants locate relevant material while concentrating influence in whoever selects the sources, configures the task, or controls the presentation of alternatives. Institutions must therefore be able to examine not only the answer produced, but also the process by which the question and available options were framed.

## 4.4 Adversarial analysis and institutional contestation

A proposal can be clearly composed and still rest on a mistaken assumption, incomplete evidence, conflicting obligation, or contested interpretation. An adversarial function can assist by seeking counterarguments, overlooked stakeholders, possible conflicts, and alternative readings of the evidence.

This function should be distinguished from **institutional adjudication**. An AI-generated challenge may identify a possible conflict, but the consequence of that challenge depends on the institution’s rules and the authority of the relevant human body. A technical mechanism might implement an applicable prohibition where the institution has validly authorized that mechanism. An AI interpretation of an ambiguous rule should not automatically be treated as equivalent to an authoritative adjudication.

Neither a separate agent label nor an apparent disagreement between two systems establishes independence. Systems may share models, providers, source collections, incentives, or assumptions. Assessing adversarial scrutiny therefore requires examining the relevant sources of dependence and the practical opportunity for human participants or independent reviewers to challenge the analysis.

Contestatory assistance is also distinct from a formal right of appeal. An institution may provide criticism before a decision while offering no effective route to challenge the resulting action. Conversely, a review procedure may exist without the resources needed to use it. The framework treats these as related but separate institutional conditions.

## 4.5 Active institutional memory

Institutions retain decisions, policies, mandates, commitments, exceptions, and other records over time. The difficulty is not only storing those records, but making relevant records accessible when an institution considers a new proposal or reviews an earlier action.

AI-assisted memory may help participants locate a prior decision, identify a possible relationship between records, or retrieve materials relevant to a current question. Provenance models such as PROV-DM provide a vocabulary for describing entities, activities, and relations among records (World Wide Web Consortium [W3C], 2013). Their use does not by itself establish that a record is authentic, complete, current, or authoritative.

A distinction is required between:

- an institutional record;
- information about the record’s provenance, status, and possible supersession; and
- an AI-generated interpretation of what that record means for a current decision.

A system can retrieve an obsolete decision, omit a later amendment, disclose information that should not be shared, or present a model-generated summary as though it were a binding precedent. Effective use of institutional memory therefore depends on governance of sources, access, status, interpretation, and correction. The appropriate mechanisms will vary by institution and by the sensitivity of the records involved.

## 4.6 Cognitive assistance and legitimate institutional decision-making

The four functions may shape a decision before any formal vote or authorization occurs. Composition can frame the problem; analysis can select evidence and alternatives; contestation can determine which objections are visible; and memory can influence which prior commitments appear relevant.

This creates a distinction between **formal decision authority** and **practical influence over a decision**. A human body may retain the formal right to decide while relying heavily on a system that shapes the available information. That possibility does not establish that such a transfer of influence has occurred in a particular institution. It does, however, define an institutional question that cannot be answered solely by asking whether the system possesses a vote.

Meaningful human participation may require more than the presence of an approval step. Candidate conditions include access to relevant evidence, time and competence appropriate to the task, the ability to request alternatives or challenge interpretations, and practical authority to reject, suspend, or escalate an action. The adequacy and proportionality of these conditions remain subject to institutional and empirical examination.

## 4.7 Risks and competing interpretations

Four risks are particularly relevant to the proposed cognitive functions.

**Agenda capture:** assistance may privilege particular problems, sources, or alternatives. Influence can arise through selection and framing even without formal voting power.

**Cognitive dependence:** participants may rely on generated explanations without independently checking matters that require their judgment. The conditions under which assistance improves or weakens independent judgment remain an empirical question.

**Epistemic monoculture:** systems presented as independent may rely on similar models, sources, or assumptions. This can produce correlated omissions or reinforce one interpretation across an institution.

**False interpretive authority:** generated summaries or apparent rule interpretations may be treated as binding decisions or verified precedents, even when they are provisional or wrong.

Possible responses include accessible source records, opportunities for alternative analysis, disclosure of relevant dependencies, and review procedures proportionate to consequences. These are design options for examination. None guarantees independent judgment, neutrality, or effective contestation.

# 5. The Three-Level Governance Framework

The three levels below distinguish different analytical questions. They do not prescribe organizational departments, software services, or a single institutional hierarchy.

## 5.1 Level I — Institutional principles and legitimate authority

Level I concerns the basis on which an institution recognizes decision rights and allocates competencies. Its questions include:

- Who may establish or amend a mandate?
- Which body has competence over the matter?
- What procedures and constraints apply?
- How can authority itself be challenged or revised?

A Level I analysis identifies an asserted authority basis. It does not assume that the institution, its founding rules, or every decision made under them is beyond challenge.

## 5.2 Level II — Governance processes and human–AI interaction

Level II concerns the processes through which institutional matters are formulated, analyzed, deliberated, authorized, delegated, monitored, and contested.

The cognitive functions discussed in Section 4 principally operate at this level, though they depend on institutional authority and technical arrangements. A process can include human participants, AI assistance, standing operational mandates, review bodies, or other arrangements suited to the institution.

A formal process may satisfy its written requirements while remaining inaccessible, poorly informed, or practically unchallengeable. Thus, procedural compliance and meaningful participation should be examined separately.

## 5.3 Level III — Technical infrastructure and implementation mechanisms

Level III concerns the mechanisms through which institutional processes may be represented or supported. Possible mechanisms include identity systems, permissions, workflow tools, evidence stores, audit records, agent runtimes, cryptographic credentials, smart contracts, or distributed ledgers.

No listed mechanism is a universal prerequisite. A technical permission can help implement an institutional rule but may be broader, narrower, outdated, or otherwise inconsistent with the mandate it represents. A technical record can support reconstruction but does not by itself establish the truth of its contents or the legitimacy of the action recorded.

## 5.4 Relationships across the levels

The levels can be used to examine whether an institutional claim at one level is adequately connected to another.

| Relationship | Question for analysis |
|---|---|
| **Authority → process** | Is the process grounded in a decision right or mandate recognized under the relevant institutional arrangements? |
| **Process → technical mechanism** | Does the configured mechanism represent the scope and conditions of the process it is intended to support? |
| **Technical action → institutional record** | What can available evidence establish about what happened, and what remains uncertain? |
| **Record → review** | Can the appropriate participants or reviewers obtain and interpret relevant evidence in time to exercise their roles? |
| **Review → correction** | What forms of suspension, revocation, correction, reversal, or remedy are available, and to whom? |

These relationships are **proposed analytical requirements**, not verified properties of any implementation. They should be applied proportionately: the evidence and review needs of a routine, low-consequence task need not be identical to those of a consequential institutional decision.

The framework proposes that institutions examine authority provenance, delegation boundaries, attributable responsibility, reviewability, proportionality, and the availability of correction. Their exact form, exceptions, and enforcement remain questions for the relevant institution and for future evaluation.

# 6. Accountable Delegation and Institutional Decision Episodes

## 6.1 The institutional decision episode

An *Institutional Decision Episode* is a bounded analytical representation of a decision or delegated action. It connects the institutional basis for an action to the cognitive and operational processes around it, available evidence, and possible review.

For purposes of analysis, an episode may be represented as:

\[
\mathcal{E}=\langle P,R,M,C,T,A,E,Q,D\rangle
\]

where:

- \(P\): the asserted institutional principal or body;
- \(R\): applicable institutional rules and decision procedures;
- \(M\): the mandate or claimed authorization;
- \(C\): the relevant technical or operational capabilities;
- \(T\): the configured technical permissions;
- \(A\): the action, proposed action, or observed event;
- \(E\): the evidence available about the episode;
- \(Q\): the practical conditions for challenge or review; and
- \(D\): the actual or possible institutional disposition.

**Mandate conformity** is represented explicitly as a derived assessment:

\[
MC(A,M,R)=\text{assessment of whether action }A\text{ conforms to mandate }M
\text{ under the applicable rules }R.
\]

This is an analytical relation, not a claim that conformity is always mechanically decidable. Where the mandate is invalid, ambiguous, missing, or contested, the assessment should record that limitation. An action may conform to the **nominal text** of a purported mandate while the authority to issue that mandate remains in question.

The representation is a conceptual aid, not a required software protocol, a complete account of institutional legitimacy, or evidence that any Axodus system implements the model.

## 6.2 Three diagnostic categories

The framework distinguishes three candidate categories. They may occur separately or together.

### 6.2.1 Authority validity concern

An action may be technically possible, permitted by a configured system, and consistent with the written terms of a purported mandate, while the mandate’s institutional basis is defective or disputed.

The analytical question is whether the body issuing the mandate had the relevant competence and followed applicable constitutive procedures. A technical permission or execution record does not answer that question by itself.

The consequences of a defect depend on the applicable institutional and legal rules. Possible responses may include suspension, further review, correction of the authority record, or consideration of ratification where permitted.

### 6.2.2 Delegated-action conformity concern

A mandate may be validly constituted while a particular action exceeds its scope or conditions. This can occur, for example, when a technical permission is broader than the mandate or when an action violates a condition that the technical system does not represent.

The analytical question is whether the action conforms to the mandate, not whether the mandate itself was validly created. Depending on the facts, review may concern configuration, operational supervision, interpretation of scope, or other institutional responsibilities.

### 6.2.3 Contestability capacity concern

A mandate may be valid and an action may conform to it, while an affected participant lacks timely evidence, access to an appropriate reviewer, or a practical opportunity to challenge or seek correction.

The analytical question is whether relevant review or corrective capacity exists in practice. A contestability concern does not automatically make the original action invalid. Nor does it establish that reversal is feasible. The institution may need to distinguish review, prospective correction, reversal where possible, and other remedies.

## 6.3 HDE-01: A hypothetical procurement episode

The following example is **fictional and nonempirical**. It illustrates how the three categories can be applied to a common episode; it does not establish effectiveness, legal consequences, or operational performance.

Assume a fictional organization whose rules permit a properly constituted committee to delegate routine purchasing within a defined category and spending ceiling. An AI-supported procurement service possesses technical credentials that can be configured to submit purchase orders. The institution also provides a review process for affected participants.

### Variant A — Authority validity

Assume that the rules require a quorum to issue a purchasing mandate, but the mandate was recorded without that quorum. The procurement service submits an order that falls within the amount configured in the technical system and within the nominal terms of the recorded mandate.

The episode raises an **authority validity concern**: technical permission and nominal conformity do not determine whether the mandate was validly constituted. A review might consider the mandate’s status, the roles involved in recording and configuring it, and whether suspension or another disposition is appropriate. Ratification or cancellation would be possible responses **only if** the applicable rules and circumstances permit them.

### Variant B — Delegated-action conformity

Assume that a valid mandate permits purchases up to **$25,000** in an approved category. A technical configuration error permits a **$29,000** order, and the service submits it.

The episode raises a **delegated-action conformity concern**: the mandate is assumed valid, but the specific order exceeds its stated ceiling. Relevant inquiry could include how the technical permission came to exceed the mandate, what supervision or checks applied, and what actions remain available. Responsibility cannot be assigned solely from the fact that the configured permission was too broad.

### Variant C — Contestability capacity

Assume that the mandate is valid, the technical permission matches it, and the service submits a **$22,000** order in the approved category. An affected participant identifies a potentially material concern, but cannot obtain the relevant evidence in time or access an effective review channel.

The episode raises a **contestability capacity concern**. The action’s conformity to the mandate does not settle whether the affected participant had a meaningful opportunity to question it. A review might examine standing, evidence access, reviewer independence, timing, and available dispositions. The purchase is not automatically invalid merely because a concern was raised, and reversal may or may not be possible.

## 6.4 What the example can and cannot establish

The variants are designed to isolate different analytical questions within one institutional setting. They suggest that review of mandate formation, review of action scope, and review of contestability may call for different evidence and involve different institutional roles.

The example does not demonstrate that the model is more effective than existing frameworks, that institutions will implement the distinctions, or that any particular remedy is legally available. Responsibility and corrective responses remain conditional on facts, institutional rules, and applicable legal context.

The categories also are not mutually exclusive in practice. A defective mandate, an out-of-scope action, and inaccessible review may occur in the same episode. Their separation is useful only to the extent that it helps an analyst avoid treating evidence of one condition as proof of the others.

# 7. Institutional Variation and Federated Autonomy

## 7.1 Conceptual applicability across institutional forms

The proposed framework is intended to support analysis across different institutional forms. Its starting point is not that all organizations possess the same authority structure, participation rules, or technical infrastructure. Rather, it asks whether the distinctions among institutional authority, governance processes, and technical mechanisms can help identify relevant questions in each setting.

This is a proposition about **conceptual applicability**. It is not evidence that the framework has been validated across institutions or that the same arrangements should be adopted in every context. Organizational constitution, applicable law, distribution of decision rights, resources, and opportunities for challenge may differ substantially.

The examples below are hypothetical configurations. They illustrate how the framework might be used; they do not define necessary characteristics of the institutions described.

### Conventional organizations and enterprises

Consider an organization in which a board establishes a purchasing policy, a management function operates within a delegated budget, and a technical service assists with supplier selection. The institution may use AI to help compose purchasing proposals, retrieve relevant contracts, identify apparent conflicts, or execute certain routine actions under a standing mandate.

The three analytical levels help separate the authority to establish the purchasing mandate, the processes through which purchasing decisions are prepared and reviewed, and the technical permissions through which orders are transmitted. The arrangement may differ in another enterprise: some decisions may be reserved to a committee, others to individual officers, and others governed by standing operational rules.

The use of AI does not determine which allocation is legitimate. Nor does a technically successful order, by itself, establish that the applicable institutional mandate was valid or followed. A further question is whether affected participants can obtain the information needed to raise a concern and reach an appropriate reviewer.

### Cooperatives and participatory institutions

A cooperative or participatory institution may organize proposal rights and decision procedures among members, representatives, or other recognized bodies. Its specific voting and participation arrangements depend on its constitution and applicable rules; no single voting formula is assumed here.

AI-assisted composition may help participants formulate proposals in a common format or locate relevant institutional records. Analysis and contestation functions may also help participants compare alternatives and identify uncertainties. These possibilities do not establish that participation becomes more equal: members may differ in access to tools, time, institutional standing, language, expertise, or influence over the agenda.

For this setting, the framework directs attention to whether assistance expands the range of proposals participants can meaningfully consider, or whether common tools concentrate interpretive power in those who configure them.

### Federated and multi-organization structures

A federation or other multi-organization arrangement may distribute authority among a common institution and constituent organizations. The relevant allocation can arise from a constitution, agreement, statute, or another institutional instrument. The degree of local autonomy, the basis of membership, and the authority of common bodies vary by arrangement; voluntary participation and uniform local powers are not assumed.

AI-assisted institutional intelligence may operate at more than one level. A local organization might use an assistant to relate a proposed policy to its own records, while a common body might use another process to examine obligations shared across the federation. A possible conflict between local discretion and a common commitment would then require interpretation under the applicable rules and by the body competent to resolve it.

Ostrom’s account of polycentric governance offers a conceptual reference for examining multiple, interdependent centers of decision-making. It does not establish that every federated arrangement is polycentric, or that an AI-assisted federation will preserve effective local autonomy (Ostrom, 2010).

### DAOs and tokenized governance arrangements

Some organizations may use decentralized or tokenized mechanisms for aspects of proposal submission, voting, or execution. These arrangements differ in how they recognize participation, allocate decision rights, and connect collective decisions to technical systems. A particular token, voting formula, smart contract, or account structure is not assumed to be necessary for every DAO.

The framework can be used to ask whether an account or technical mechanism acts under an institutionally recognized mandate, how the relevant decision procedure is justified, and what review or correction is possible. A cryptographically valid action may be important evidence of what a technical system accepted, but it does not by itself settle the institutional legitimacy of the procedure or the authority exercised.

## 7.2 Shared capabilities and constituted authority

Organizations may reuse a model, software component, analytical method, source collection, or agent configuration developed elsewhere. Such reuse may reduce duplication and allow organizations to draw on capabilities they did not build independently. Suitability, however, remains context-dependent: a capability that is useful in one setting may omit information, assumptions, or procedures needed in another.

The governing distinction is:

> **Capabilities may be shared; institutional authority is not transferred by technical reuse alone.**

A reused capability does not automatically acquire the mandates of its developer or the receiving organization. At the same time, the receiving organization and any applicable common authority may legitimately establish powers of representation, supervision, or coordination through appropriate institutional instruments. Whether such powers exist, their scope, and how they may be exercised depend on the relevant rules and authority.

The distinction also applies to cognitive influence. A shared service might possess no formal authority over a local decision while still influencing it through source selection, default wording, availability of alternatives, or control over updates. A technology-neutral governance analysis should therefore examine both **formal authority** and **practical control over institutional intelligence**.

## 7.3 Local discretion and shared commitments

Where a federation has applicable common commitments, local action may need to be considered in relation to both local rules and shared obligations. The resulting allocation of competence is an institutional question; it cannot be deduced from technical architecture alone.

An authorized technical mechanism may implement a specific rule within its established scope. For example, an institution might configure a system to refuse an action when an expressly defined condition is not met. Whether that refusal is authorized, contestable, or subject to exception depends on the institutional arrangement.

An ambiguous question presents a different problem. An AI system may identify an apparent conflict between a local proposal and a shared commitment, but the interpretation and disposition of that conflict must be attributed to the authority competent under the applicable rules. The model does not presume that every such matter requires the same body, procedure, or intervention.

Federation is consequently one setting in which authority, institutional intelligence, and delegation interact. It is not a required form of AI-assisted governance.

# 8. Counterarguments, Risks, and Limitations

The framework’s normative premise and conceptual distinctions do not ensure that an institution will preserve meaningful human governance. The following counterarguments identify conditions under which the proposed arrangement may fail or produce effects contrary to its objectives.

## 8.1 Cognitive dependence and ceremonial authority

AI-assisted analysis may make information easier to access, but it may also lead participants to defer to generated outputs. Dependence could arise where reviewers lack time, expertise, access to underlying evidence, or practical authority to reject a recommendation.

A human approval step would then record a formal disposition while leaving much of the practical framing and evaluation outside effective human scrutiny. This is a challenge to the framework’s human-authority premise: authority retained on paper may not correspond to control exercised in practice.

Human–AI interaction guidance and critiques of oversight support examining these conditions; they do not establish that cognitive decline or ceremonial approval inevitably follows every use of assistance (Amershi et al., 2019; Green, 2022). The effects of assistance on judgment and skill require context-specific evidence.

## 8.2 Agenda capture and political asymmetry

AI may influence governance before a formal decision is made. A system that selects records, recommends a problem definition, or offers a limited set of options can affect which matters receive attention and how alternatives are understood.

This influence may be concentrated in model providers, administrators, source curators, institutional officers, or participants with better access to private tools. Formal equality of voting rights may therefore coexist with unequal capacity to shape deliberation.

The proposition is not that all AI assistance produces capture. It is that a framework concerned only with formal decision rights could overlook the institutional power exercised through agenda formation, information selection, and control over cognitive resources.

## 8.3 Epistemic monoculture and correlated errors

Several AI systems may appear to provide independent assessments while relying on related models, shared source collections, similar instructions, or common institutional assumptions. Under those conditions, their errors may be correlated.

Shared foundations do not imply identical outputs or inevitable failure. Conversely, technical diversity alone does not establish substantive independence: systems built differently may still reproduce the same omission or institutional bias.

An institution considering multiple forms of AI assistance should therefore ask what kinds of dependence matter for the decision at hand and whether participants can access alternative interpretations or relevant primary evidence.

## 8.4 Limits of adversarial agents

An adversarially assigned system may identify objections that a proponent overlooked. Its label, however, does not prove impartiality, independence, competence, or sufficient authority to make an objection consequential.

An agent developed by the same team or using the same resources as a compositional system may still produce useful criticism. It may also share incentives or assumptions that narrow its challenge. The relevant question is what the adversarial function actually examines, who controls it, and whether its findings can be questioned or acted upon.

If an institution treats the existence of an adversarial agent as proof that adequate scrutiny occurred, the procedure could provide assurance without establishing that the underlying challenge was meaningful.

## 8.5 False memory and interpretive authority

AI-assisted memory systems may use document retrieval, structured databases, language models, or combinations of these methods. They can consult records, but their usefulness depends on the selection, currency, access conditions, and interpretation of those records.

A system may retrieve an obsolete policy, omit an amendment, misread the scope of a precedent, or present an inferred relationship as though it were an authoritative institutional conclusion. Participants may then mistake a generated summary for the underlying record or for a binding interpretation.

The framework therefore distinguishes retrieval of a record from a model-generated interpretation of that record. Provenance can support inspection, but it does not itself establish that the record is complete, current, or legally authoritative (W3C, 2013).

## 8.6 Institutional compliance and political merit

A proposal may satisfy the procedural rules applicable to it while remaining undesirable, unfair, wasteful, or politically contested. Equally, a proposal’s apparent merit does not cure a defect in the authority or procedure through which it is adopted.

AI-assisted analysis may help identify compliance questions and explain possible consequences. It cannot turn procedural conformity into a conclusive judgment about political merit. The distinction is especially important when system-generated summaries present one course of action as the natural or preferred result of a rule check.

## 8.7 Procedural burden and informal bypass

Evidence, review, and contestation can impose costs. If a framework requires the same depth of documentation and review for every action, participants may experience delay or workload disproportionate to the consequences of the decision.

One possible institutional response is to bypass formal procedures; another is that routine work simply becomes slower. These are risks and testable possibilities, not established outcomes of the proposed model.

Proportionality is consequently part of the framework’s design objective. The appropriate level of evidence and review requires institutional judgment and may vary with consequence, reversibility, affected parties, and available capacity. Existing work on reviewability informs the need to make processes reviewable; it does not by itself demonstrate that a particular review regime causes paralysis (Cobbe et al., 2021).

## 8.8 Evidence access and meaningful review

Providing a large volume of records does not necessarily enable effective challenge. Evidence may be late, incomplete, inaccessible, technically difficult to interpret, or unavailable to the people affected by a decision.

Reviewability should therefore be distinguished from practical contestability. An institution may have records that permit a technical audit while an affected participant lacks standing, assistance, time, or an independent reviewer. Contestability research supports examining intervention and challenge as institutional design concerns; the adequacy of a particular procedure remains to be evaluated (Alfrink et al., 2023).

## 8.9 Irreversibility and corrective limits

Some delegated actions can be suspended or corrected before their effects become durable. Others may create consequences that cannot be fully undone, even if an institution later identifies an authorization or execution failure.

The framework distinguishes **revocation** of future authority, **suspension** of activity, **correction** of a process or record, **reversal** of an action where possible, and **remedy** for consequences that remain. These concepts do not establish that every remedy is available or adequate.

Where action is difficult to reverse, an institution may choose to examine preventive review or narrower delegation. The appropriate choice depends on the decision, the relevant rights and risks, and the rules under which the institution operates.

## 8.10 Jurisdictional and legitimacy limits

The framework is a normative-conceptual account, not a universal legal theory. Institutional authority, legal personhood, agency, standing, contract effects, and liability may vary across jurisdictions.

An internal finding that an action exceeded a mandate does not automatically determine the action’s legal effect on third parties. Likewise, a procedure may be institutionally recognized while the legitimacy of the institution or procedure remains contested. Specific legal consequences require the applicable legal context and competent review.

# 9. Research Propositions and Future Evaluation

The propositions below identify possible directions for future research. They are not reported findings or authorization to conduct studies. Any later protocol would require an appropriate design, access to evidence, and relevant institutional, privacy, legal, and research-ethics review.

For each proposition, a null result or an adverse result would be informative. Measures should not be selected solely to confirm the framework.

## 9.1 Proposal composition

**Hypothesis.** AI-assisted composition may change participants’ ability to prepare proposals that satisfy specified procedural requirements.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Correct identification of required information, omission of relevant alternatives, participant drafting time, and errors introduced by the assistance.

**Possible comparison.** Participants using a standard template could be compared with participants using AI assistance on equivalent, synthetic proposal tasks.

**Confounders and limitations.** Prior expertise, language, accessibility, tool familiarity, task design, and the quality of the underlying rules may affect results. Procedural completeness does not establish proposal merit.

**Evidence required.** A defined protocol, independent assessment criteria, records of omissions and introduced errors, and reporting of positive, null, and negative effects.

## 9.2 Cognitive access, understanding, and political influence

**Hypothesis.** Providing shared institutional intelligence may affect participants’ understanding and their ability to identify relevant alternatives, but may also introduce common framing effects.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Comprehension of source material, recognition of omitted alternatives, confidence calibration, and differences in participants’ opportunity to influence an agenda.

**Possible comparison.** Compare source-only, standard-summary, and AI-assisted conditions using the same decision materials.

**Confounders and limitations.** Reading ability, prior knowledge, trust, accessibility, source quality, and differences between simulated influence and actual institutional power. Improved comprehension would not by itself establish political equality.

**Evidence required.** Transparent measures, independent review of framing, analysis across participant groups where appropriate, and documentation of null or adverse effects.

## 9.3 Adversarial contestation

**Hypothesis.** Structured adversarial assistance may alter the identification of conflicts, omissions, or unsupported claims during deliberation.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Relevant issues identified, false objections, missed objections, participant workload, and participants’ willingness to challenge the adversarial analysis itself.

**Possible comparison.** Compare deliberation without an adversarial brief, with a human-produced brief, and with an AI-assisted brief, where such comparisons are feasible and ethically approved.

**Confounders and limitations.** Expertise, topic familiarity, brief length, shared source material, model dependence, and institutional incentives. A greater number of objections is not necessarily better scrutiny.

**Evidence required.** Independently defined issue criteria, assessment of false positives and omissions, and examination of whether challenges lead to meaningful institutional consideration.

## 9.4 Institutional memory

**Hypothesis.** AI-assisted retrieval and presentation of institutional records may affect participants’ ability to locate and correctly interpret relevant prior decisions.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Retrieval precision, identification of superseded records, accuracy of source interpretation, time to locate relevant material, and disclosure or access errors.

**Possible comparison.** Compare conventional search and AI-assisted retrieval on a controlled collection containing current, amended, superseded, and irrelevant records.

**Confounders and limitations.** Quality and completeness of archives, access permissions, changes in rules over time, and the distinction between finding a record and determining its authority.

**Evidence required.** A documented source set, independently verified status of records, error analysis, and privacy and access controls appropriate to the material.

## 9.5 Decision-episode reconstruction and failure detection

**Hypothesis.** A representation based on the Institutional Decision Episode may affect reviewers’ ability to reconstruct an action and distinguish mandate, conformity, and contestability concerns.

The representation to be evaluated would use the accepted Stage 1 notation:

\[
\mathcal{E}=\langle P,R,M,C,T,A,E,Q,D\rangle,
\qquad MC(A,M,R).
\]

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Reconstruction accuracy, correct identification of missing evidence, classification of the three concerns, reviewer disagreement, time, and cases that do not fit the categories.

**Possible comparison.** Independent reviewers could examine equivalent synthetic records presented as conventional case files and as episode-structured materials.

**Confounders and limitations.** Reviewer training, familiarity with the notation, case complexity, quality of the underlying evidence, and the risk that the structured format directs reviewers toward predetermined categories.

**Evidence required.** Independently prepared cases and scoring criteria, reporting of disagreements and unclassifiable cases, and comparison of workload as well as accuracy. A faster or more consistent classification would not by itself demonstrate institutional legitimacy or effective remedy.

## 9.6 Delegation and practical intervention

**Hypothesis.** Explicit representation of a mandate, its operational boundaries, and available review conditions may affect whether reviewers can identify a mismatch between institutional authorization and observed action.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Detection of mandate-scope discrepancies, recognition of unavailable intervention, correct distinction among suspension, revocation, reversal, and remedy, and uncertainty reported by reviewers.

**Possible comparison.** Use hypothetical episodes in which mandate validity, technical permission, action conformity, and review capacity vary independently and in combination.

**Confounders and limitations.** Ambiguous rules, incomplete evidence, institutional variation, and the artificial simplicity of controlled scenarios. A hypothetical mismatch is not evidence that any actual system contains the same defect.

**Evidence required.** Clearly stated assumptions, cases with overlapping or uncertain failures, independent review of the classifications, and documentation of situations in which the framework adds no useful distinction.

## 9.7 Cross-institutional applicability

**Hypothesis.** The three analytical levels and four foundational dimensions may help organize comparable questions across more than one institutional form, while requiring adaptation to local rules and practices.

**Candidate observable outcomes.** Whether analysts can identify authority, process, technical mechanisms, cognitive functions, and relevant review conditions without omitting important institution-specific features.

**Possible comparison.** Apply the framework to a small, purposively selected set of institutional cases and compare its account with alternative conceptual descriptions.

**Confounders and limitations.** Unequal access to institutional records, jurisdictional differences, case selection, translation of local terminology, and the possibility that the framework forces unlike arrangements into misleading categories.

**Evidence required.** Explicit case-selection criteria, access and confidentiality arrangements, attention to counterexamples and residual categories, and a clear boundary between conceptual comparison and generalizable empirical claims.

# 10. Conclusion

This paper proposes an integrative conceptual framework for examining how human institutions may incorporate AI into governance while preserving legitimately constituted authority, meaningful participation, accountability, and institutional autonomy.

The framework distinguishes four substantive dimensions. **Human legitimacy** concerns the institutional basis of decision rights and authority. **Institutional intelligence** concerns the cognitive work through which proposals are composed, evidence is considered, claims are contested, and institutional memory is made available. **Agentic delegation** concerns the relationship among mandates, operational capability, technical permission, action, and responsibility. **Federated institutional autonomy** concerns how distinct institutions may organize their own capabilities while observing applicable shared commitments; federation remains an application context rather than a universal requirement.

The three analytical levels distinguish institutional authority, governance processes, and technical mechanisms. The Institutional Decision Episode connects these levels around a bounded action or decision. Its separation of mandate validity, delegated-action conformity, and contestability capacity is an integrative device for examining different institutional concerns. The hypothetical procurement episode illustrates how those concerns may lead to different questions about evidence, responsibility, and possible responses; it does not demonstrate that the device improves governance or determines legal outcomes.

The framework also faces substantial counterarguments. AI assistance may shape agendas, encourage dependence, reproduce shared errors, or present generated interpretations as institutional authority. Formal human approval, source records, or a named adversarial function do not independently resolve those risks. Conversely, extensive review can impose burdens that require proportionate institutional judgment.

The paper therefore advances a conceptual and normative research direction. Its practical usefulness, empirical effects, and applicability across institutional forms remain open to evaluation. Its contribution is framed as a synthesis of established concerns rather than as a claim of distinctive originality.

> **Intelligence may be distributed; authority must be constituted.**
